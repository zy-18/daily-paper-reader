# Deep catastrophic-risk-aware reinforcement learning ∗

Abstract 

Estimating extreme risk in reinforcement learning becomes increasingly difficult as the confidence level increases, since only a small number of observations contribute to empirical estimates of Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). In this paper, we extend an Extreme Value Theory (EVT) approach for CVaR optimization to high-dimensional neural-network policies. Using a peaks-over-threshold representation and method-of-moments estimation, we obtain a differentiable tail-risk objective and show that, conditional on the selected threshold, its finite-sample gradient can be computed directly through automatic differentiation. We first validate the approach in a controlled experiment with a known optimal solution and then investigate its performance in a deep-hedging problem with rare extreme market movements. Our results show similar hedging behavior for EVT- and empirical-CVaR-trained policies under normal conditions, while EVT-trained policies exhibit lower hedging losses under the extreme-scenario evaluation across the training sample sizes considered. These results suggest that EVT can provide a practical alternative for training neural policies when the observations relevant to extreme risk are scarce. 

Keywords: Extreme Value Theory; Reinforcement Learning; Policy Gradient; Tail Risk.

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a flexible framework for sequential decision problems in which actions are selected to optimize an objective over a multi-period horizon. In risk management applications, however, optimizing average performance may not adequately account for rare but potentially severe losses. Risk-aware RL addresses this problem by considering objectives such as the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), which explicitly focuses on the tail of the loss distribution. The estimation of such objectives becomes increasingly difficult at high confidence levels, since only a small fraction of the observations generated during training directly characterize the extreme tail. Extreme Value Theory (EVT) provides a natural framework to address this scarcity of tail obser-vations. Under the peaks-over-threshold (POT) approach, observations exceeding a sufficiently high threshold are modeled using a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD), allowing threshold exceedances to be used to extrapolate the behavior of the extreme tail and estimate extreme risk measures; see, e.g., Troop et al. (2021). Building on this idea, Davar et al. (2025) introduces an EVT-based policy-gradient methodology in which POT estimates of CVaR are incorporated into the optimization of risk-aware RL policies. Their numerical results show the potential benefits of the approach when observations in the extreme tail are scarce, particularly for heavy-tailed loss distributions. The methodology of Davar et al. (2025), however, relies on finite differences to approximate the gradient of the EVT-based objective with respect to the policy parameters. This requires repeatedly evaluating the objective after modifying individual policy parameters, making the procedure increasingly computationally restrictive as the number of policy parameters grows. This limitation concerns primarily the dimension of the policy parameterization rather than the dimension of the underlying sequential decision problem. Extending the POT framework to neural-network policies would therefore allow EVT-based tail-risk optimization to be combined with the flexibility offered by deep RL. In this paper, we investigate this extension by constructing an EVT-based loss that can be 2differentiated with respect to the parameters of a neural-network policy. The proposed approach estimates the tail of the loss distribution using the POT methodology and propagates the resulting tail-risk objective through the policy using automatic differentiation. We complement the proposed methodology with a theoretical analysis of the differentiability and statistical properties of the EVT-based objective. In particular, the analysis distinguishes the local differentiability of the finite-sample EVT objective from the statistical consistency of the corresponding tail-risk estimator under explicit assumptions on the excess distribution. We study the methodology numerically in two stages. We first consider a controlled experiment in which the optimal policy is analytically tractable. This setting allows us to compare the numerical behavior of the empirical and EVT-based optimization procedures against a known solution and to examine how their performance changes as the amount of information available for estimation increases. We then consider neural-network policies, using deep hedging as the principal application. Deep hedging provides a natural setting for this analysis since the terminal hedging error defines a loss distribution whose adverse tail is directly relevant for risk management, while the policy itself can be represented by a neural network Buehler et al. (2019). Our analysis therefore extends EVT-based policy optimization from low-dimensional policy parameterizations relying on finite-difference gradients to neural-network policies trained through automatic differentiation. The theoretical analysis investigates the conditions supporting this extension, while the numerical experiments examine its behavior first in a controlled environment and subsequently in a deep-hedging setting. In the latter, we investigate whether explicitly modeling the tail during training provides an advantage over empirical CVaR optimization as losses become more extreme and tail observations increasingly scarce. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the risk-aware rein-forcement learning problem, the EVT framework for tail-risk estimation, and the EVT-based policy optimization approach on which our methodology builds. Section 3 presents the proposed extension to neural-network policies and the corresponding theoretical results. Section 4 presents the numerical study, beginning with a controlled experiment and subsequently considering the 3deep-hedging application. Finally, Section 5 concludes.

## 3 EVT-Based Optimization of Neural-Network Policies 

The EVT-based policy optimization framework introduced in subsection 2.3 relies on the finite-difference approximation in Equation 2.11. While suitable for low-dimensional policy parameteri-zations, its computational cost increases with the number of policy parameters, limiting its direct application to neural-network policies. We address this limitation by formulating the POT-MOM estimator in Equation 2.9 as a differentiable empirical objective. ## 4 Numerical Study 

We now investigate the numerical behavior of the methodology developed in Section 3. We first consider a controlled experiment for which the optimal policy is known analytically, and subsequently move to a deep-hedging problem where the policy is represented by a neural network 14 and catastrophic losses arise from rare market scenarios.

We first investigate whether replacing empirical CVaR with the EVT-based objective affects the training behavior of neural-network policies when extreme observations are not especially prominent. To account for the stochasticity of neural-network optimization, we train otherwise identical feedforward policies over four independent runs and compare their validation CVaR throughout 50 training epochs. The resulting trajectories are presented in Figure 2. A clear difference appears during the initial stages of training. The EVT-based objective exhibits a faster reduction in mean validation CVaR, while the empirical approach decreases more gradually. This difference is particularly pronounced during approximately the first 15 epochs. The dispersion across independent runs is also considerably smaller for the EVT-based approach during this period, whereas the empirical-CVaR policies exhibit greater variability in their validation trajectories. After approximately 20 epochs, however, the mean validation CVaR of both approaches becomes similar and remains close throughout the remainder of training. This suggests that the EVT-based objective induces more stable optimization behavior during the early training stage, which is consistent with the policies reaching the region of their eventual validation performance with fewer training iterations. The experiment therefore indicates that the main difference between the two objectives in this setting concerns the dynamics and stability of the optimization rather than the validation risk eventually attained. This behavior is consistent with the statistical motivation of the proposed estimator: when relatively few observations determine the empirical tail-risk objective, variation in those observations can propagate directly into the optimization step, whereas the POT approximation uses the exceedance distribution to estimate the relevant tail quantity. Once training progresses and both policies reach a similar region of the parameter space, the difference between their validation performance becomes substantially smaller. This raises a more demanding question for the proposed methodology: whether the difference between the two objectives becomes more consequential when policy performance is evaluated specifically in the extreme tail of the loss distribution. 18 Figure 2: Validation CVaR 0.999 throughout training under the empirical and EVT-based opti-mization approaches. 1 10 20 30 40 50

For the present experiment, we set p = 0 .999 , such that E0.999 corresponds to market realizations in the upper 0.1% tail of the unhedged option-payoff distribution. We consider two independent out-of-sample evaluation samples. The first consists of 100,000 paths generated directly from the original market distribution. The second is constructed by independently simulating additional paths from the same market model and retaining complete trajectories belonging to E0.999 until 3,000 such trajectories are obtained. Thus, the second sample concentrates the evaluation on rare and severe terminal market outcomes while leaving the underlying data-generating process unchanged. For brevity, we refer to this conditional extreme-event sample as the heavy-tail regime throughout the remainder of the numerical study. 4.5 Sensitivity to Training Sample Size 

The previous results show that the difference between empirical-CVaR and EVT-based opti-mization becomes more pronounced when the learned policies are exposed to extreme scenarios. 21 This raises a related question: whether this behavior depends on the amount of data available during training. This is particularly relevant at the catastrophic confidence level α = 0 .999 , where only a small fraction of the simulated losses directly determines the empirical tail-risk objective. We therefore examine whether reducing the number of training paths materially changes the out-of-sample behavior of the resulting policies. We train the empirical-CVaR and EVT-based policies using 50 ,000 , 100 ,000 , 200 ,000 , and 400 ,000 

simulated paths while maintaining the remaining training specifications unchanged. Each trained policy is subsequently evaluated on 100,000 out-of-sample paths from the general distribution and on the 3,000-path conditional extreme-scenario sample defined in subsection 4.4. Figure 4 reports the resulting distributions of out-of-sample hedging errors. Under the general distribution, shown in Panel A, the hedging-error distributions remain relatively stable as the number of training paths changes. The empirical-CVaR and EVT-based policies also exhibit broadly comparable out-of-sample behavior at each training-sample size. In particular, reducing the training sample from 400 ,000 to 50 ,000 paths does not produce a clear deterioration in the central part of either distribution. Thus, within the range of sample sizes considered here, the general out-of-sample performance appears relatively insensitive to the amount of training data. The heavy-tail evaluation in Panel B provides a different perspective. Across all four training-sample sizes, the EVT-trained policies exhibit hedging-error distributions shifted toward smaller values relative to their empirical-CVaR counterparts. Importantly, this separation remains visible when the number of training paths is reduced. At the same time, the distributions do not exhibit a clear monotonic improvement as the training sample increases from 50 ,000 to 400 ,000 .The experiment therefore does not support the stronger claim that increasing the number of training trajectories systematically improves either policy over this range. ## 5 Conclusion 

This study extends the EVT-based risk-aware reinforcement learning framework considered in the baseline methodology to policies parameterized by neural networks. The main limitation of the original approach in this setting arises from its reliance on finite-difference gradients, whose computational cost increases with the number of policy parameters. We address this limitation by expressing the POT-CVaR estimator as a differentiable empirical objective and computing its gradient jointly with respect to the neural-network parameters through automatic differentiation. The resulting framework preserves the EVT treatment of extreme losses while making it applicable 23 to substantially richer policy parameterizations. The theoretical results establish the properties supporting this construction. In particular, for a fixed threshold and under the stated local regularity conditions, the finite-sample POT-MOM objective is locally differentiable and automatic differentiation recovers its exact gradient. Moreover, as the number of threshold exceedances increases, the POT-MOM estimator converges in probability to its corresponding population POT-CVaR functional under the assumptions established in Section 3.3. These results characterize the differentiability and statistical behavior of the EVT component used within policy optimization, while keeping distinct the approximation of the true CVaR and the optimization of the neural-network parameters. The numerical experiments complement these results from two perspectives. First, the controlled experiment provides an analytically tractable benchmark and shows that the proposed gradient-based procedure approaches the known optimal policy parameters. Second, the deep-hedging experiment illustrates the behavior of the methodology in a substantially more complex neural-network optimization problem. Under the general out-of-sample market distribution, the EVT and empirical-CVaR approaches ultimately provide comparable hedging performance, although the EVT-based objective exhibits a faster initial reduction and lower between-run variability in validation CVaR during training. The distinction becomes more pronounced under the conditional extreme-scenario evaluation: the EVT-trained policies exhibit substantially smaller hedging losses, while performance under the general market distribution remains broadly comparable. Together, these results suggest that explicitly modeling the loss tail can be particularly useful when neural-network policies are trained to manage rare but severe outcomes. The benefit is therefore not that EVT uniformly improves policy performance, but rather that it uses the exceedance distribution to model the tail in regimes where empirical CVaR relies on relatively few observations beyond the target quantile. The EVT-based training approach consists of three main steps. First, the threshold above which the loss distribution is approximated by a GPD is selected using a sequence of Anderson–Darling goodness-of-fit tests combined with the ForwardStop procedure. Second, conditional on the selected threshold, the CVaR training objective is estimated using the POT-MOM estimator when sufficient exceedances are available, with empirical CVaR used as a fallback otherwise. Third, the resulting objective is differentiated with respect to the neural-network parameters using automatic differentiation. Threshold selection is performed outside the differentiation step, and the selected threshold is treated as fixed while the active finite-sample objective is differentiated.

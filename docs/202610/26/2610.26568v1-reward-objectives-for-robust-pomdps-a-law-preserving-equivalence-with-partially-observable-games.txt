# Reward Objectives for Robust (PO)MDPs: A Law-Preserving Equivalence with (Partially Observable) Games 

Durgam Latha Indian Institute of Technology Bombay Mumbai, India Dion Reji Indian Institute of Technology Bombay Mumbai, India Ðor ¯ de Žikeli´ cNanyang Technological University Singapore, Singapore Shankaranarayanan Krishna Indian Institute of Technology Bombay Mumbai, India 

Abstract 

Robust partially observable Markov decision processes model agents that act under par-tial observability while adversarial environment resolves uncertainty in the transition probabilities. Reward objectives such as discounted-sum, long-run average and cumu-lative reward under finite and infinite horizons allow quantifying the utility of an agent. We consider the problem of solving RPOMDPs under all four reward objectives, where the goal is to decide whether the value of a risk measure with respect to a reward objec-tive exceeds a given threshold. We consider different risk measures, such as (1) expected value, (2) mean-variance (MV), (3) conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), and (4) entropic risk (ERisk). Our results can be summarized as follows. First, we formalize the class of law-invariant risk measures , that subsume all four risk measures above. Second, we propose a unified framework for solving RPOMDPs with discounted-sum, long-run average and cumulative reward objectives under any law-invariant risk measure, by re-ducing the problem to solving partially observable stochastic games (POSGs). Our unified framework applies to (s, a )-rectangular RPOMDPs with polytopic uncertainty sets, which we show to be equivalent to POSGs, for each of the four reward objec-tives and any law-invariant risk measure. We prove equivalence by designing logspace reductions between the RPOMDP and POSG models, that map strategies to strategies and preserve the distribution of the reward objective, up to a constant rescaling factor. Third, as a corollary, we derive new computational complexity results for R(PO)MDPs and POSGs. In particular, we show that solving (s, a )-rectangular RMDPs is as hard as solving simple stochastic games. 

# 1 Introduction 

Partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) are the standard model of an agent act-ing under partial observability in the presence of stochastic uncertainty [23]. The key limitation of POMDPs, however, is that their transition probabilities are assumed to be fully known. This is a strong assumption in the settings where transition probabilities are e.g. estimated from data. Robust MDPs (RMDPs) [22, 34, 51] and, more generally, robust POMDPs (RPOMDPs) [37, 6], account for this by replacing fully known transition probabilities with an uncertainty set of tran-sition probabilities, with the adversarial environment being able to choose transition probabilities from this uncertainty set. Recent years have seen significant interest in solving R(PO)MDPs with reward objectives, such as discounted-sum reward [35, 22, 24, 47, 17, 20, 2] or long-run average reward objectives [49, 19, 18, 50, 9, 32]. The goal in solving an RPOMDP is to compute a strategy that maximizes the expected value of the reward objective. Existing works largely focused on de-signing efficient algorithms for solving RMDPs. Solving RPOMDPs, which also account for partial observability, has been considered in [6, 37, 7, 46, 33, 13, 16, 25]. 1arXiv:2610.26568v1 [cs.AI] 19 Oct 2026 These works all focus on computing a strategy that maximizes the expected value (E) of the reward objective. However, while providing valuable information on the average-case performance of the strategy, expected value alone does not consider the variability of possible outcomes. Two strategies that achieve the same expected reward objective value may carry very different risks. This is why expected value is sometimes replaced by other risk measures such as mean-variance [30], conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) [42, 10], or entropic risk [21, 3]. However, computing strategies that optimize these risk measures has so far mostly been studied for MDPs, but not for robust models such as RMDPs or RPOMDPs. The goal of this work is to address this gap. We present a unified framework for solving RPOMDPs with reward objectives and different risk measures , by reducing the problem to solving partially observable stochastic games (POSGs). Our framework applies to discounted-sum, long-run average and total reward under finite and infinite horizons objectives, and under any law-invariant risk measure. A risk measure is said to be law-invariant , if it only depends on the probability distribution of the value of the reward objective over RPOMDP runs. We show that common risk measures such as (1) expected value ( E), (2) mean-variance (MV), (3) conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), and (4) entropic risk (ERisk) are all law-invariant. Hence, our unified framework applies to all these risk measures. We consider (s, a )-rectangular RPOMDPs with polytopic uncertainty sets, which we show to be 

equivalent to POSGs. For each of the four reward objectives and under any law-invariant risk mea-sure, we design two logspace reductions: one from RPOMDPs to POSGs, and one from POSGs to RPOMDPs. Our reductions map strategies to strategies and are law preserving , meaning that the im-age of every strategy achieves the same distribution of the reward objective value (up to the reward objective being normalized by a constant rescaling factor), thus mapping optimal strategies to opti-mal strategies under any law-invariant risk measure. Hence, we can reduce the problem of solving an RPOMDP to solving a POSG under the same reward objective and risk measure and vice-versa. In the case of RMDPs (with full observability), our reductions establish equivalence with turn-based stochastic games (TBSGs) [44, 11]. Building upon our equivalence result, we study the threshold problems for law-invariant risk mea-sures such as E, MV , CVaR , and ERisk with rational parameters, and for the finite and infinite horizon total, long-run average and discounted-sum reward objectives. We consider the non-strict and strict threshold problems: given a model (RPOMDP/RMDP/POSG/TBSG), a risk measure Ψ ,a reward objective X, and a rational t, decide whether the agent in the RPOMDP/RMDP (or the maximizing player in POSG/TBSG) can guarantee a value of Ψ on the reward objective X that is greater than t (strict threshold), or greater or equal than t (non-strict threshold). Under partial observation, both variants are PSPACE -hard and decidable for finite horizon total reward, and undecidable for infinite horizon total reward and for long-run average reward objectives. For discounted-sum rewards, values can be approximated to any precision, which makes the strict problem recursively enumerable; it is nevertheless undecidable for 

E and CVaR , while for discounted mean-variance and entropic risk the decidability of the threshold problems remains open. Under full observation, both variants are SSG-hard for RMDPs with total or long-run average rewards for all four risk measures, and for discounted-sum rewards for the ex-pected value. For the expected discounted-sum and the long-run average reward, the SSG bound is tight, since both problems lie in NP ∩ coNP . Here, SSG stands for the complexity of solving simple stochastic games, which is known to be in NP ∩ coNP [11] but the question of whether there exists a polynomial algorithm is an open problem. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 1. Law-invariant risk measures. We formalize law-invariant risk measures for reward objectives in RPOMDPs. These include (1) expected value ( E), (2) mean-variance (MV), (3) conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), and (4) entropic risk (ERisk). 2. Equivalence of RPOMDPs and POSGs. We present a unified framework for solving RPOMDPs with reward objectives and law-invariant risk measures , by reducing the problem to solving 2POSGs. Our framework applies to (s, a )-rectangular polytopic RPOMDPs with discounted-sum, long-run average, total and finite-horizon reward objectives. It is based on our novel 

equivalence result between RPOMDPs and POSGs obtained via logspace reductions in both directions. In the case of RMDPs (with full observability), we obtain equivalence with TB-SGs. 3. Uniform complexity landscape. We derive new computational complexity results and pro-vide a complexity landscape for RPOMDPs (and POSGs) under reward objectives and law-invariant risk measures.

# 3 Law-Invariant Risk Measures 

In this work, we study the problem of solving RPOMDPs under the reward objectives defined above, where the goal is to calculate the value of a risk measure of the reward objective . To that end, we first formally define a class of risk measures that we consider. We then define the problem of solving RPOMDPs (and, analogously, POSGs) under these risk measures of the above reward objectives .Let Ω be the set of plays in an RPOMDP M or a POSG G, and let X : Ω → R be a reward objective which is either the total reward until time N (X = Tot N ), the total reward ( X = Tot ), the discounted-sum reward ( X = Disc γ ), or the long-run average reward ( X = LimAvg ). ## 4.2 From ac-POSGs to RPOMDPs 

In the other direction, the environment in the RPOMDP takes the role of the min -player in the ac-POSG. Two points need care. First, in the ac-POSG, min moves in a state t of its own, after observing 

Zmin (t). In contrast, in the RPOMDP, the environment moves in the same step as the agent, with no intermediate state, and hence no observation, in between. To address this discrepancy, in our reduction we turn every min -state t into a state dt of the RPOMDP in which the agent has a single dummy action ⊤ and the environment, after observing Zmin (t), chooses the next distribution from the polytope spanned by min ’s options. # 5 The Complexity Landscape 

By Theorem 1, every complexity bound for the threshold problem for each of the reward objectives and any law-invariant risk measure transfers between RPOMDPs and ac-POSGs (for average and discounted returns, with the rescaling by the constant c of Lemma 1). We now use this result to derive new complexity bounds for R(PO)MDPs and ac-POSGs under reward objectives; Table 1 summarizes them. All numbers in the input (probabilities, rewards, discount factors, thresholds, and parameters) are rationals encoded in binary, so that every instance has a finite description. ## 5.1 Bernoulli profiles 

Our hardness results are reductions from decision problems about the probability of an event in a simpler model: whether a POMDP strategy avoids a bad event with probability 1 over a finite horizon [38] (Theorem 2); whether some POMDP strategy reaches a target with probability above b

or all strategies stay below a, a gap problem derived from probabilistic automata [12] (Theorem 3); and whether a simple stochastic game reaches its target with probability above 12 [11] (Theorem 5). In each reduction we turn the instance into a reward model whose return equals v > 0 if the event occurs and 0 otherwise. For the finite-horizon total reward we pay v once at the last step if the event has occurred; for the total reward we pay v once when the absorbing target is entered; for the long-run average we pay v in every step spent in the target. This does not work for discounted returns, where the return also depends on when the event occurs, so discounted-sum rewards are treated separately. A strategy under which the event has probability p then yields the return law Ber v (p) = (1 −

p)Dirac (0) + p Dirac (v) for p ∈ [0 , 1] . A law-invariant risk measure Ψ sees only this law, so the value of the strategy is Ψ (Ber v (p)) , a function of p alone. If this function is nondecreasing in p, the best strategy is the one that maximizes p. A threshold for Ψ then becomes a threshold on the optimal probability, which is the question the source problem asks. We therefore only need to know how Ψ

behaves on the laws Ber v (p), which motivates the following notion. The Bernoulli profile of a law-invariant risk measure Ψ is the function βΨv (p) = Ψ (Ber v (p)) for

## 5.2 Partial observation 

Lower bounds. Lower bounds for POMDPs apply to RPOMDPs directly, since a POMDP is an RPOMDP with singleton uncertainty sets, and they apply to ac-POSGs by our equivalence in Theorem 1. 

Theorem 2 (Finite horizon total reward) . If the Bernoulli profile of a law-invariant risk measure 

Ψ is strict at the top, then the finite-horizon total reward threshold problem for Ψ (both strict and non-strict) is PSPACE -hard, already for POMDPs; hence it is PSPACE -hard for RPOMDPs and ac-POSGs. Proof sketch. [38] show that it is PSPACE -hard to decide whether a POMDP with nonnegative costs has a strategy of expected total cost 0 over the first N ≤ | S| steps, i.e., one that avoids all pairs with positive cost with probability 1. Their result concerns the expected cost, and it is a question about the probability pσ of avoiding the costly pairs. We show that, for every law-invariant risk measure 

Ψ whose Bernoulli profile is strict at the top, the threshold problem for Ψ encodes exactly this question. To this end, we add a step counter and collect reward v exactly when step N is completed without a costly pair, so that the total return has law Ber v (pσ ), where pσ is the success probability of σ. By compactness, max σ pσ exists, so the value is βΨv (max σ pσ ). Strictness at the top gives 

βΨv (p) < β Ψv (1) for p < 1, so the value is at least the rational threshold βΨv (1) if and only if some strategy succeeds with probability 1. Hence the threshold problem for Ψ is PSPACE -hard for POMDPs, by a reduction computable in logarithmic space. The result transfers to RPOMDPs, of which POMDPs are a special case, and to ac-POSGs by the forward construction of Section 4, which is computable in logarithmic space and preserves the laws of the finite-horizon total return (with the horizon doubled and no rescaling of the rewards). Thus, the hardness of [38] for expected cost is lifted to all risk objectives with a strict Bernoulli profile, and to both RPOMDPs and ac-POSGs. For infinite horizon total reward and long-run average reward, we use the undecidability of proba-bilistic automata with a gap [12]. Lemma C.2 in Appendix C.4 translates the result to POMDPs: for rationals 0 < a < b < 1, one computes from a two-counter machine a POMDP with a single observation and an absorbing target T such that some strategy reaches T with probability greater than b if the machine halts, while every strategy, randomized or not, reaches T with probability at most a otherwise.

# 6 Related Work 

The study of robust MDPs goes back to [22, 34]. Efficient algorithms for solving RMDPs under expected discounted-sum rewards were proposed in [35, 22, 51, 24, 47, 17, 20], and under expected long-run average rewards in [49, 19, 18, 50, 9, 32, 2]. Robust POMDPs were introduced by [37], and [6] proposed game semantics under partial observability. The relationship between RPOMDPs and POSGs is not a novel observation. [9] showed that RMDPs under expected discounted-sum and long-run average rewards can be reduced to TBSGs, and [6] showed that RPOMDPs can be reduced to POSGs. Most recently, [28] showed that RPOMDPs and POSGs are equivalent (i.e. inter-reducible) under omega-regular objectives, a class of logical objectives including reachability, safety, and linear temporal logic objectives, but which does not consider reward objectives. The novelty of our work is that RPOMDPs and POSGs are equivalent for reward objectives and every law-invariant risk measure . Our reductions are law preserving : they map strategies to strategies that induce the same probability distribution of the reward objective, up to a normalizing constant factor that depends only on the type of the reward objective. Law invariance is a basic property of risk measures [27, 15]. Variance-based criteria in MDPs are studied in [45, 14, 30, 40]; CVaR in [42, 10, 26, 39, 31]; and entropic risk in [21]. For TBSGs, they are studied in [3]. Time-consistent dynamic risk measures are developed in [43].The works [36, 10] relate risk sensitivity to robustness in MDPs, however they do not propose complexity results or algorithms for solving RMDPs with reward objectives under these risk measures. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We showed that robust POMDPs and alternating-control POSGs are equivalent in a strong, distri-butional sense. We designed logspace reductions between the RPOMDP and ac-POSG models, that map strategies to strategies and preserve the distribution of the reward objective, up to a constant factor, for any of our four reward objectives and any law-invariant risk measure. This allows us to derive a number of new computational complexity results for R(PO)MDPs. Several questions remain open: the exact complexity of finite-horizon total reward RPOMDPs be-yond PSPACE -hardness; the decidability of the non-strict threshold problems for discounted E and 14 CVaR α, and of both threshold problems for discounted mean-variance and entropic risk, under par-tial observation; and upper bounds for mean-variance and CVaR in robust MDPs, where only SSG-hardness is known. It would also be interesting to extend the equivalence beyond (s, a )-rectangular uncertainty.

# Appendix 

Recall that at the end of section 2.1, we assume all actions are available at all the states of POSG. In this appendix, we present reductions from one model to the another (RPOMDP to POSG and vice-versa), as well as POSGs with prescriptions to ordinary POSGs. In some of these proofs, while 19 going from one model to the other, the set of actions are expanded, and in some transitions we use only the newly defined actions. Indeed, as seen in the main paper, the remaining actions are not “missing”, one can always complete the transitions by directing the transitions on the remaining actions to a sink which is losing for the owner of the said state. The actions that are depicted on transitions from state s (prior to completion) are the effective ones, in the same spirit as [28]. To ease our notations in proofs, we define A(s) to denote the set of effective actions at s.

Discounted returns. Lemma A.1 covers Tot N , Tot and LimAvg because these returns do not depend on exactly when a reward is earned, apart from the horizon, which we adjust, and a constant factor for the average. A discounted return does: the reward of the t-th transition has weight γt.The chain gadget delays the exit reward of every min -visit and makes one round take 2k instead of 2

transitions. To preserve discounted returns for every γ, we need a compilation that keeps the timing of G. The idea is to give min the whole prescription as a single action: the available actions at all states of an observation class are the tuples that contain one action for each state of the class, and the state that is actually visited uses its own component. This costs an exponential blow-up of the action sets, but changes neither the states nor the timing.

Step 3: the reduction. By Step 2, Val G ≥ c if and only if Val MG (E, Disc γ ) ≥ c, and MG is a polytopic RMDP with the same binary discount factor, computable in logarithmic space from 

G. Given a polynomial-time Turing reduction from SSGs to the threshold problem for discounted TBSGs, we replace each oracle query (G, c ) by the query (MG, c ) to the threshold problem for discounted RMDPs. This is a polynomial-time Turing reduction from SSGs to the latter problem. The same holds for the strict threshold problem, because the values coincide: any reduction from SSGs to a variant of the TBSG threshold problem yields a reduction to the same variant for RMDPs. 51

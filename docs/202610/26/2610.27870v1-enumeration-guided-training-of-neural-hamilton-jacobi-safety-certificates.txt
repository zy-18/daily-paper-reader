# ABSTRACT 

Hamilton–Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis offers formal safety guarantees for control systems, but training accurate neural certificates remains difficult, particularly when unsafe regions are hard to characterize. We propose a verification-guided training framework that addresses this by using probabilistic enumeration of safety constraints to detect forward-invariance violations, i.e., states where the learned certificate incorrectly classifies unsafe behavior. This produces a compact, targeted set of counterexamples that more precisely captures the failure boundary than inputs generated by standard adversarial attack methods. Crucially, using this representation to fine-tune a pretrained HJ value function yields larger certified regions and reduces certification time across all tested benchmarks. We further introduce a shielding mechanism based on the refined certificates to enforce safety constraints at deployment time. We evaluate the proposed safety filter in a realistic, physics-based Isaac Sim environment and through real-world autonomous racing experiments, achieving zero safety constraint violations compared to a nominal controller and a backup policy based on Linear Quadratic Regulation (LQR). These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed shielding mechanism in ensuring safe operation under high-speed, safety-critical conditions. 

# 1 Introduction 

For control systems interacting with the physical world, safety is a primary concern in controller design, as controllers must satisfy safety constraints at all time steps over an infinite horizon. Consider, for example, a robot locomotion task where safety requires maintaining a positive clearance from surrounding obstacles at all times. Two distinct challenges arise in such settings. The first is identifying the set of states from which long-term constraint satisfaction is guaranteed, the so-called feasible region , since the deployment of a controller should be restricted to this set. The second is actively enforcing safety at runtime, as even when a controller is initialized within the feasible region, it may issue actions that push the system toward constraint violations, and an explicit safety mechanism is required to intercept and correct such actions before they are executed. Addressing both challenges simultaneously is essential for deploying safety-critical controllers in realistic environments. A widely adopted approach to enforce safety constraints is based on Model Predictive Control (MPC) [1]. Nominal MPC incorporates state and input constraints directly into a finite-horizon optimal control problem solved repeatedly online. Under suitable assumptions, nominal MPC can exhibit a degree of robustness; however, when hard state constraints are present, these safety margins can become very small. To account for bounded disturbances and model uncertainty, robust MPC variants such as tube-based methods [2, 3] confine the system trajectory to a tube around the nominal trajectory, ensuring constraint satisfaction through tightening of the nominal problem. Nonetheless, constraint tightening can introduce conservatism that degrades performance in demanding tasks, and the finite planning horizon limits the scope of safety guarantees that can be provided. A more principled framework for characterizing safety over an infinite horizon is Hamilton–Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis [4], which offers a formal setting for computing feasible regions of control systems subject to safety constraints. HJ reachability defines the feasible region as the zero-sublevel set of a value function V (x), representing the worst-case constraint violation along an optimally controlled trajectory originating from state x. Compared with MPC-based arXiv:2610.27870v1 [cs.RO] 20 Oct 2026 Figure 1: Overview of the proposed enumeration-guided training and online safety filter pipeline. approaches, HJ reachability provides direct global safety guarantees rather than relying on finite-horizon optimization and repeated replanning. This makes V a natural candidate not only for certifying the feasible region, but also for constructing a runtime safety filter, namely a shield , that corrects unsafe control actions by substituting a verified backup policy whenever a nominal controller would violate the safe set boundary. However, this increased rigor comes at a high computational cost, as the underlying HJ partial differential equations suffer from the well-known curse of dimensionality, making exact solutions intractable for high-dimensional systems. To address this limitation, several recent works have proposed neural HJ certificates, leveraging function approximation to scale reachability analysis to more complex systems [5, 6]. Nevertheless, learned certificates do not inherently guarantee that the resulting zero-sublevel set defines a sound feasible region and therefore require formal verification to confirm that safety guarantees are preserved. This limitation motivated the approach of Yang. et al. [7], which integrates neural value function learning within a training loop guided by adversarial search and formal verification. While this approach improves scalability and enables learning of provably safe certificates, it faces three critical challenges that we aim to address in this work. First, the adversarial procedure is empirical and relies on sparse, locally generated counterexamples as gradient-based attacks explore only a narrow neighborhood of the current iterate and may therefore miss failures located in other regions of the state space. As a result, the search may report no violation even when unsafe states remain in the fine-tuned certificate, not because these states are genuinely safe, but because the search did not reach them. This false negative prematurely triggers the formal verifier, which, being NP-hard [8], can incur substantial computational cost. The verifier then identifies the violation that the adversarial search missed, generates additional counterexamples, and initiates another fine-tuning cycle. This process can repeat over many iterations, leading to significant verification overhead before convergence. The inefficiency becomes particularly acute in high-dimensional systems, where the local coverage of adversarial search can be small relative to the global extent of the failure set. Second, even after a verified certificate has been obtained, the question of how to exploit it as a practical runtime safety filter for a learned controller operating in a complex environment remains unaddressed: translating the certified feasible region into an effective shielding mechanism requires additional design choices that the learning procedure alone does not resolve. This paper addresses both limitations by introducing a novel enumeration-guided training strategy depicted in Fig. 1. At the core of our approach is the enumeration of compact unsafe regions, which replaces purely local counterexample search with a more structured exploration of the state space based on preimages of the unsafe set in deep neural networks [9, 10]. The underlying intuition is that isolated local violations provide only limited information about the geometry of the failure set, whereas counterexamples drawn from a globally organized, spatially distributed exploration better characterize its shape and extent, offering a richer training signal to the learning algorithm. Crucially, our enumeration-guided training is not a substitute for formal verification; it improves the quality, diversity, and coverage of the counterexample set during training, while verification retains its role as the provable safety certification. In addition to this new training strategy, and building on the least-restrictive safety framework of Fisac et al. [11], we introduce a shielding mechanism that leverages the refined neural HJ certificate to enforce safety at deployment time. The proposed shield acts as a safety filter between a nominal controller and the plant: at each time step, it checks whether the intended action preserves forward invariance of the certified safe set and, if necessary, overrides it with a safe recovery action provided by a backup policy based on Linear Quadratic Regulation (LQR) [12]. Unlike existing HJ-based safety filters, which typically rely on grid-based value functions or learned approximations without formal safety guarantees, our 2approach introduces a formally verified neural HJ certificate that, by construction, ensures forward invariance of the certified safe set under the verified system dynamics. This enables the deployment of formally certified safety filters in complex systems where closed-form safety certificates are unavailable. Our empirical evaluation spans four safe-control verification benchmarks from [7], covering systems with both linear and nonlinear dynamics, as well as a new obstacle-avoidance benchmark based on the RoboRacer platform (formerly F1TENTH) [13]. Enumeration-guided training yields faster convergence times and, in general, larger certified regions across all benchmarks, with the largest gains on higher-dimensional and more complex tasks. The verified RoboRacer certificate is first evaluated in a realistic, physics-based Isaac Sim environment to assess its robustness against different track geometries, corridors, and heading disturbances. We further validate its effectiveness under real-world conditions by deploying it on a physical RoboRacer platform on an unseen real racing circuit. This deployment confirms the effectiveness of the proposed solution for complex safety-critical tasks. 

# 2 Background 

2.1 Neural Hamilton-Jacobi Certification 

To synthesize formally verified neural approximations of Hamilton-Jacobi value functions, we follow the verification-guided learning pipeline introduced in [7]. 

Definition 1 (Closed-loop system and safety specification) . Let x ∈ X ⊆ Rn denote the system state. We consider a discrete-time closed-loop dynamical system 

xt+1 = f π (xt), (1) 

where f π : X → X represents the system dynamics under a fixed control policy π. Let h : X → R be a safety function, and define the safe set as 

S := {x ∈ X | h(x) ≤ 0} (2) The objective is to learn a function whose zero-sublevel set characterizes a certified safe region of the state space. 

Definition 2 (Neural value function and zero-sublevel set) . Let Vθ : X → R be a neural network parameterized by θ.The associated zero-sublevel set is defined as 

Cθ := {x ∈ X | Vθ (x) ≤ 0} (3) The goal is to synthesize Vθ such that Cθ is a feasible region , i.e., a subset of the safe set that is forward invariant under the closed-loop dynamics. Formally, this requires the following properties to hold for all x ∈ X :

Vθ (x) ≤ 0 ⇒ h(x) ≤ 0, (constraint satisfaction) (4) 

Vθ (x) ≤ 0 ⇒ Vθ (f π (x)) ≤ 0, (forward invariance) (5) The synthesis pipeline proceeds as follows. First, neural models of the closed-loop dynamics f π and the safety function 

h are learned from sampled data. These models are then used to initialize and pre-train the value network Vθ [7]. During pre-training, the value network is calibrated to shrink the initial zero-sublevel set, producing a conservative candidate region that reduces the burden of formal verification in later stages. Subsequently, the model is refined through a verification-guided fine-tuning process. Specifically, a projected gradient descent (PGD)-based counterexample search [14] identifies violations of (4)–(5), while periodic mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) verification [15] attempts to formally certify the candidate set. While this pipeline enables the achievement of a formally verified HJ safety certificate, it may still suffer from limitations due to its reliance on sparse counterexamples, which provide only localized feedback to the learning process. In contrast, counterexamples obtained through more global and spatially structured exploration can better capture the failure set geometry, thereby offering richer guidance for refining the value function and enabling potentially easier post hoc verification. 

2.2 Neural Network Preimage Bounds Computation 

Verifying properties (4) and (5) of a neural certificate Vθ amounts to characterizing the set of inputs that a deep neural network maps into a specified output region, a problem known as preimage computation . Formally, given a network f : RN → R, an input domain X ⊆ RN , and an output set Y ⊆ R, which together define a verification query 

T = ⟨f, X , Y⟩ , the neural network preimage bounds computation, as known as AllDNN-Verification problem [9, 10, 16], asks for the exact preimage set Γ( T ) =  x ∈ X f (x) ∈ Y . In our setting, the relevant query concerns the violation set of the current certificate, Γθ := {x ∈ X | Vθ (x) ≤ 0 ∧ max {h(x), V θ (f π (x)) } > 0}, i.e., the states at which (4) 3Figure 2: Comparison between PGD attack and approximate enumeration of unsafe regions (coverage c = 95% ) for a pretrained V (x) on a toy example. or (5) fails, so that Vθ satisfies (4)–(5) if and only if Γθ = ∅. However, computing this preimage set exactly is #P-hard [9] and is therefore computationally infeasible in general under standard complexity assumptions. To circumvent this barrier, recent works have introduced approximate and/or probabilistic preimage representations [17, 18, 19, 20]. Rather than recovering Γ( T ) exactly, these methods construct a finite collection R = {r1, . . . , r m} of polytopes that provides a user-specified coverage ratio c ∈ (0 , 1] , with a prescribed confidence level in case of probabilistic approaches. In the next section, we show how an enumeration-based strategy inspired by these approximate preimage methods can be embedded directly into the verification-guided training loop for neural HJ certificates. The resulting procedure replaces sparse adversarial search with a more structured exploration of the boundary of Cθ (as shown in Fig. 2), while providing probabilistic coverage guarantees and thereby reducing the number of verification-guided training iterations. Importantly, the enumeration only needs to cover the violation set, not to be contained in it: every state sampled from R

is checked exactly against (4)–(5) before being used for training, so regions that include non-violating states only reduce sampling efficiency, whereas missed regions would leave violations unaddressed. We also stress that the enumeration step is used only to guide training, not to establish the final safety guarantee: the fine-tuned certificate is still subjected to formal verification, which provides the final provable certification. 

# 3 Methodology 

3.1 Enumeration-guided training 

While the overall verification objective remains unchanged from that proposed in [7], namely enforcing constraint satisfaction and forward invariance of the zero-sublevel set of Vθ , we strengthen the search for violations by introducing an explicit enumeration phase that enables more global exploration of the state space. At a high level, instead of relying solely on sparse counterexamples, we proactively identify regions of the state space that are likely to violate either condition (4) or (5). Given the current value function Vθ, this amounts to characterizing subsets of the failure set, i.e., states x ∈ X such that either Vθ (x) ≤ 0 ∧ h(x) > 0 or Vθ (x) ≤ 0 ∧ Vθ (f π (x)) > 0. However, as previously discussed, precisely identifying these regions is computationally intractable: even for small piecewise-linear neural networks, enumerating the preimages of such conditions requires reasoning over an exponential number of activation patterns, making the problem #P-hard [9]. To address this limitation, we adopt a probabilistic enumeration strategy inspired by [19], approximating the failure set up to a desired coverage c via randomized yet structured exploration of X .Specifically, we construct an ensemble of randomized decision trees trained on bootstrapped samples of the state space, where each sample is labeled according to whether it violates constraint satisfaction or forward invariance under the current value function Vθ . Each tree partitions the input space into axis-aligned regions, providing a local approximation of the failure set. By combining predictions from the entire ensemble, we detect regions that are consistently classified as violating and that together cover at least a fraction c of the true unsafe volume with high confidence and bounded error [19]. Notably, these regions form a spatially coherent approximation of the failure set, which can be efficiently sampled to extract informative counterexamples. This process enables the learning algorithm to exploit a richer and more structured representation of violations, capturing the geometry of the failure set rather than isolated points. When the enumerated regions no longer provide sufficient valid counterexamples, we resort to a neighborhood-based fallback strategy that locally perturbs previously identified violating states, densifying the exploration around the critical boundary regions of the current zero-sublevel set Cθ . If no new counterexamples are found for a prescribed number of iterations, we invoke the MILP verifier to assess whether the current Vθ candidate satisfies properties (4)–(5) over the entire domain X . If certification succeeds, the procedure terminates. If a counterexample is returned, it is added to the buffer and the refinement process resumes. If the verifier is inconclusive, training proceeds without certification. Algorithm 1 formalizes the proposed procedure. It takes as input an initial pretrained value network Vθ, the learned closed-loop dynamics f π , the safety function h, the state space X , and enumeration with verification parameters. At each of the K max iterations, the algorithm proceeds as follows. First, every fenum steps, it recomputes candidate unsafe regions via EnumUnsafe , implemented using the probabilistic exploration described above. Then, if the buffer is insufficiently populated (i.e., fewer than Bmin samples), new n samples are drawn uniformly from these regions and filtered to retain only valid counterexamples. If no such samples are available, a neighborhood-based fallback search is performed, sampling nf b new samples, and whenever the buffer contains valid violations, the V is updated. Otherwise, a counter is incremented, and when it exceeds smax , formal verification is triggered to either certify the current Vθ candidate or provide additional counterexamples. By combining probabilistic global exploration with local refinement, the proposed method provides more informative supervision to guide the optimization of Vθ and enables faster verification of the final certificate. 

3.2 Runtime safety filter via neural HJ certificates 

Once a certificate Vθ satisfying (4)–(5) has been obtained, it can be deployed as a safety filter , or shield . Specifically, our filter wraps an arbitrary nominal policy πnom and modifies its behavior only when necessary. Following [7], verification is performed for a fixed backup policy πb on a ReLU network approximation ˆf πb of the closed-loop dynamics f πb (x) = f (x, π b(x)) , i.e., (4)–(5) hold for ˆf πb . In our setting, πb is a saturated LQR controller represented by a fixed ReLU network. Since the certificate is learned rather than derived analytically, the shield applies to systems for which closed-form safety certificates are unavailable. The exactness assumption reflects the verification setting of [7]; since the nominal branch is checked with the known dynamics f , approximation errors of ˆf πb affect only the backup branch and could be absorbed by verifying (5) with a Lipschitz-based margin. 

# 4 Empirical Evaluation 

To validate the proposed enumeration-guided refinement strategy, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation across simulated and real-world scenarios. Specifically, we investigate three research questions: (RQ1) How does enumeration-guided HJ training affect the quality of the resulting safety certificate? (RQ2) Can our approach improve training efficiency by reducing the wall-clock time required to obtain a provably verified certificate? and (RQ3) Can the verified neural HJ certificate be effectively deployed as a runtime safety filter to enforce safety in a realistic safety-critical task? 

To answer these questions, for each task described below, we compare the state-of-the-art fine-tuning strategy of [7], which uses a PGD attack to generate counterexamples followed by formal verification of the neural HJ certificate using the MILP verifier [15], with our proposed enumeration-guided refinement strategy. We refer to these approaches as PGD+MILP and Enum+MILP , respectively. We evaluate the two approaches using three key metrics. First, the true feasible rate (TFR) [7] measures the fraction of truly feasible states contained in Cθ , thereby quantifying the quality of the certified region. It is estimated on 100 ,000 states sampled uniformly from X , where a state is deemed truly feasible if the closed-loop trajectory under πb starting from it incurs no constraint violation over 100 steps. Second, we measure the number of MILP calls and their corresponding times. For all methods, we set a fixed timeout of 150 min. The neural HJ certificate Vθ, whose zero-sublevel set C = {x : Vθ (x) ≤ 0} defines the certified safe region, is represented by a neural network with two hidden layers, each containing 32 ReLU units. Under the fixed backup policy, a MILP verifier [15] exhaustively verifies both constraint satisfaction and one-step forward invariance, i.e., conditions (4)–(5). To account for the variability introduced by neural network initialization, we train five certificates from three independent random initializations for each method and task. Hence, we report the mean and standard deviation across these three runs, providing a more robust assessment of the performance of the compared refinement strategies. Furthermore, we note that both the baseline approach of [7] and our proposed enumeration-guided strategy are sensitive to hyperparameter choices. Hence, we tune each method independently using CARBS [22] under the same protocol and budget, maximizing TFR with the number of MILP calls as the cost metric; wall-clock and final verification times are recorded separately and not optimized. The experiments were conducted on AMD EPYC 9355 CPUs and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. The code and final configurations used to collect the results are available on github. 4.1 Answer to RQ1 and RQ2 - Simulation results 

Table 2 reports our simulation results. In general, the proposed enumeration-guided fine-tuning strategy enables larger TFR certificates with fewer verification calls and less computational time. This is due to the fact that, unlike PGD, which returns individual adversarial states and thus provides sparse, pointwise supervision, our enumerator identifies entire unsafe regions from which multiple violations can be sampled during fine-tuning. This exposes the value network to a broader portion of the failure set at each refinement step and reduces the risk of repeatedly correcting isolated counterexamples while nearby violations remain unresolved. Nonetheless, CartPole results clarify the current limitation of our enumeration approach. Its nonlinear dynamics induce curved boundaries (as shown in Fig. 4) that are only coarsely represented by the axis-aligned hyperrectangles used by our enumerator. Consequently, the returned regions may contain substantial portions that are uninformative for refinement, reducing the efficiency of the region-level supervision and leading to a smaller final certified region. This limitation concerns the particular geometric abstraction adopted in our implementation, rather than the enumeration-guided refinement principle itself, which is agnostic to the representation used to characterize unsafe regions.

4.2 Ablation study - On the impact of enumeration 

To assess the contribution of identifying compact representations of counterexamples in reducing the number of steps to convergence (i.e., iterations ), we introduce a Random Sampling+MILP baseline that replaces unsafe-region enumeration with uniform sampling. Specifically, at each fine-tuning iteration, the baseline samples n states uniformly from X and retains only those violating (4) or (5), following the same counterexample filtering procedure (FilterCE) as 

Enum+MILP . Both methods use identical buffer sizes, update rules, and MILP verification schedules, with a maximum budget of 5000 iterations. Thus, they differ only in the strategy used to generate candidate counterexamples. As shown in Table 3, Enum+MILP achieves higher TFR while reducing the number of iterations required to obtain a verified certificate. We evaluate this effect on two benchmarks of increasing complexity: Pendulum and Robot Arm. On Pendulum, Enum+MILP reaches verification in 1563 ± 111 iterations, compared to 1934 ± 506 for random sampling. The advantage becomes particularly pronounced on Robot Arm, where uniform sampling fails to obtain a verified certificate within the 5000 -iteration budget, whereas Enum+MILP successfully converges in 2129 ± 81 iterations, achieving a TFR of 69 .14 ± 0.60 . These findings demonstrate that explicitly identifying unsafe regions provides a more informative refinement signal than uniformly sampled counterexamples, accelerating convergence and improving the effectiveness of the fine-tuning process, particularly on more complex benchmarks. 4.3 Answer to RQ3 - HJ certificate deployment 

To assess the robustness of the verified HJ certificate under realistic operating conditions, we first evaluate it in a physics-based Isaac Sim environment integrated with ROS2, considering different track geometries, sensing uncertainties, and disturbances affecting the perceived free-space corridor and vehicle heading. Specifically, we introduce a 0.11 s steering 9delay, modeled by propagating the previously issued control and evaluating the safety filter (6) at the predicted state. We deploy the verified certificate as a safety filter around a pure-pursuit nominal controller πnom [25], which tracks obstacle-avoidance paths generated by a planner within the available corridor. The backup policy πb is implemented as an LQR that steers the vehicle toward the corridor center. When the predicted state falls outside Cθ or the operating limits are exceeded, braking is applied as a recovery action. # 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced a verification-guided training framework for neural Hamilton–Jacobi certificates based on probabilistic enumeration of unsafe state-space regions. By providing diverse, region-level counterexamples, our approach offers richer refinement guidance than local gradient-based searches, leading to larger certified HJ value function regions, while achieving substantially faster convergence times on all tested benchmarks. Crucially, our approach enables the synthesis of certificates on complex tasks where pointwise counterexamples fail. Our real-world experiment demonstrated that the verified neural HJ certificate obtained using the proposed strategy can support safe obstacle avoidance while preserving nominal driving between safety interventions. Building on these promising results, future work will focus on extending this framework to address more challenging control tasks, such as those with high-dimensional state spaces, or improving the enumeration with semantically richer geometric representations, e.g., zonotopes, oriented bounding boxes, or polyhedra. 10 Acknowledgments
